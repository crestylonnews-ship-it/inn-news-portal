  # Webb Telescope Reveals Ancient Origin of 3I/ATLAS Interstellar Comet
  
  > An international team used the James Webb Space Telescope to analyze the third interstellar comet, 3I/ATLAS, finding extremely high deuterium content and low carbon-13 ratios, suggesting it formed in an ancient region of the Milky Way 10 to 12 billion years ago, making it a cosmic fossil from the universe's "noon" period.
  
  Paris Observatory – A research team at PSL University published an important study on the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS in the journal Nature on June 22, 2026. The team integrated observation data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) to make the first detailed measurement of the chemical composition of this third confirmed interstellar object.
  
  The study found that 3I/ATLAS has an abnormally high deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio and a significantly low carbon-13 isotope ratio. These characteristics differ from any comet in the solar system, indicating that the object formed in an extremely ancient and cold region of the Milky Way, with an estimated age of 10 to 12 billion years, possibly born during the "cosmic noon" period when star formation in the universe was most vigorous.
  
  According to announcements from the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Webb Space Telescope, multiple international astronomical institutions confirmed that after 3I/ATLAS moved away from the Sun in December 2025, JWST's NIRSpec instrument successfully captured its precise chemical signals. Both NASA's Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) and ESA science releases emphasized that the comet's deuterium content is approximately 30 times that of solar system comets, with this data formally published following peer review in the journal Nature.
  
  The Paris Observatory team further pointed out that the low carbon-13 ratio combined with the high deuterium ratio points to an extremely low-temperature (approximately minus 260 degrees Celsius) molecular cloud environment. Such conditions exist only in the early Milky Way or in the outer regions of neighboring galaxies, making 3I/ATLAS a key sample for studying galactic chemical evolution and the distribution of prebiotic molecules.
  
  Media reports are highly consistent on the core findings, though details of interpretation vary slightly. The Paris Observatory press release explicitly mentions that the "international team includes researchers from the Paris Observatory," while ESA and NASA releases focus on instrument data and age estimates without particularly emphasizing team composition. Additionally, both ESA and STScI use the term "cosmic noon" to describe the peak star formation period, but the Paris Observatory source does not directly adopt this terminology, instead referring only to an "ancient region." Regarding the temperature and density conditions of the formation environment, all sources point to extreme cold but do not provide specific numerical values; the fact of low carbon-13 ratios is confirmed by all sources.
  
  What remains to be clarified is whether 3I/ATLAS indeed originated from the outer regions of the Milky Way or may have come from frozen clouds of another galaxy—a point that requires more spectral observations for verification.
  
  This discovery has direct implications for astronomy and planetary science. First, the chemical composition of 3I/ATLAS provides a reference baseline for solar system formation theories, showing that solar system comets have relatively lower deuterium content, which may reflect evolutionary differences in different nebular regions. Second, the high-deuterium environment suggests that organic molecules essential for life may have been widely present in the early galaxy, aiding assessments of conditions for the origin of life in other regions of the Milky Way. For space agencies, this research reinforces the irreplaceability of the Webb Telescope in interstellar object chemical analysis, and future similar missions will prioritize configuring infrared spectrometers. On a societal level, public interest in the concept of "cosmic fossils" may increase support for fundamental astronomical research.
  
  On the other hand, some scholars caution against overinterpreting the chemical data from a single comet. While deuterium and carbon isotope ratios are indicative, the sample is limited to one object and may not represent the general conditions of ancient regions of the Milky Way. The Paris Observatory team emphasized this is a "preliminary inference" that requires combining observations of other interstellar objects to build a complete model. Furthermore, the relationship between star formation rates and chemical abundances during the "cosmic noon" period remains theoretically controversial, and the age estimate of 3I/ATLAS relies on isotope decay models that may be influenced by the physical conditions of the primordial cloud. No opposing views or data contradictions were mentioned in media reports, but the scientific community generally believes that more independent observations are needed to verify the conclusions.
  
  Future observation priorities will focus on three directions: first, whether future telescopes (such as the European Extremely Large Telescope or the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope) can detect spectral features of similar interstellar objects; second, whether orbital backtracking of 3I/ATLAS can precisely pinpoint its origin nebula; and third, laboratory simulations of extremely low-temperature molecular cloud chemical reactions to compare with observational data. The Paris Observatory team stated that follow-up research is being planned, which will combine higher-resolution ALMA data to analyze the comet's dust composition. The International Astronomical Union may also discuss updates to interstellar object classification standards at next year's conference. The public can track the latest developments through ESA and NASA official channels, and the related paper is openly accessible.

# 韋伯望遠鏡揭示3I/ATLAS星際彗星古老起源

> 國際團隊利用詹姆斯·韋伯太空望遠鏡分析第三顆星際彗星3I/ATLAS，發現其氘含量極高且碳十三比例偏低，推測形成於100億至120億年前的銀河系古老區域，為宇宙「正午」時期留下的宇宙化石。

巴黎天文臺－PSL大學研究團隊於2026年6月22日《自然》期刊發表針對3I/ATLAS星際彗星的重要研究，該團隊整合詹姆斯·韋伯太空望遠鏡（JWST）與阿塔卡瑪大型毫米波／次毫米波陣列（ALMA）的觀測資料，首次詳細測量此第三顆確認星際天體的化學組成。
研究指出，3I/ATLAS的氘與氫比例異常偏高，碳十三同位素比例則顯著偏低，這些特徵與太陽系內任何彗星均不相同，顯示該天體形成於銀河系極其古老且寒冷的區域，年齡估計達100億至120億年，可能誕生於宇宙恆星形成最旺盛的「宇宙正午」時期。
根據歐洲太空總署（ESA）與韋伯太空望遠鏡官方發布，多個國際天文機構共同確認3I/ATLAS在2025年12月遠離太陽後，JWST的NIRSpec光譜儀成功捕捉其精確化學訊號。NASA史密松天體物理中心（STScI）與ESA科學發布均強調，該彗星的氘含量約為太陽系彗星的30倍，此資料由《自然》期刊同行審查後正式發表。
巴黎天文臺團隊進一步指出，低碳十三比例與高氘比值共同指向極低溫（約攝氏零下260度）的分子雲環境，這種條件僅存在於銀河系早期或鄰近星系的外圍區域，使3I/ATLAS成為研究星系化學演化與生命前驅分子分佈的關鍵樣本。
各媒體報導在核心發現上高度一致，但細節詮釋略有差異。巴黎天文臺新聞稿明確提及「國際團隊包含巴黎天文臺研究人員」，而ESA與NASA發布則側重儀器資料與年齡推算，未特別強調團隊構成。此外，ESA與STScI均使用「宇宙正午」一詞描述恆星形成高峰期，但巴黎天文臺來源未直接採用此術語，僅說明「古老區域」。關於形成環境的溫度與密度條件，各來源皆指向極端寒冷，但未提供具體數值；碳十三比例偏低的事實則為所有來源共同確認。
目前尚待釐清的是，3I/ATLAS是否確實源自銀河系外圍，或可能來自其他星系的凍結雲團，此點需更多光譜觀測驗證。
此發現對天文學與行星科學具有直接影響。首先，3I/ATLAS的化學組成為太陽系形成理論提供對照基準，顯示太陽系彗星的氘含量相對較低，可能反映不同星雲區域的演化差異。其次，高氘環境暗示生命必需有機分子可能在星系早期已廣泛存在，有助於評估銀河系其他區域的生命誕生條件。對太空機構而言，此研究強化了韋伯望遠鏡在星際天體化學分析上的不可替代性，未來類似任務將更優先配置紅外光譜儀。社會層面，公眾對「宇宙化石」概念的關注可能提升對基礎天文研究的支援意願。
不同觀點方面，部分學者提醒不宜過度解讀單一彗星的化學資料。氘與碳同位素比例雖具指示性，但樣本僅限一顆天體，未必代表銀河系古老區域的普遍條件。巴黎天文臺團隊強調此為「初步推測」，需結合其他星際天體觀測才能建立完整模型。此外，「宇宙正午」時期的恆星形成速率與化學豐度關係仍存在理論爭議，3I/ATLAS的年齡推算依賴同位素衰變模型，可能受原始雲團物理條件影響。媒體報導中未提及任何反對意見或資料矛盾，但科學界普遍認為需等待更多獨立觀測以驗證結論。
後續觀察重點將聚焦於三項方向：一是未來望遠鏡（如歐亞地面極大望遠鏡或南希·格雷斯·羅曼太空望遠鏡）是否可偵測類似星際天體的光譜特徵；二是3I/ATLAS的軌道回溯能否精確指向其起源星雲；三是實驗室模擬極低溫分子雲化學反應，以比對觀測資料。巴黎天文臺團隊表示正規劃後續研究，將結合ALMA更高解析度資料分析彗星塵埃成分。國際天文聯盟亦可能於明年會議中討論星際天體分類標準的更新。公眾可透過ESA與NASA官方頻道追蹤最新進展，相關論文已開放取閱。
