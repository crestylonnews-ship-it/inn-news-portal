  # WHO and Africa CDC Expand Surveillance Partnership, but Health Workforce Shortage Remains Unresolved
  
  > The World Health Organization, Africa CDC, and Germany's RKI are expanding the "Partnership for Health Security Surveillance in Africa" across seven countries; meanwhile, a report shows Africa's health workforce has grown to 5.72 million but covers only 46% of demand, with shortages and unemployment coexisting.
  
  The World Health Organization (WHO) recently announced an expansion of its existing partnership with the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) and Germany's Robert Koch Institute (RKI) to implement the "Partnership for Health Security Surveillance in Africa" across seven countries on the African continent. This move aims to strengthen regional epidemic surveillance and response capabilities, addressing the long-standing public health challenges faced by Africa.
  
  At the same time, the WHO African Region released a new report indicating that Africa's health workforce grew from 4.3 million in 2018 to 5.72 million in 2024, but the growth rate still fails to meet actual needs. Only 46% of the regional health workforce gap has been filled, and the continent faces the contradictory phenomenon of severe workforce shortages coexisting with high unemployment rates.
  
  According to the WHO's official release, the expanded partnership focuses on strengthening collaboration in disease surveillance systems. By integrating the expertise of the WHO, Africa CDC, and RKI, specific projects will be advanced in seven African countries. The Africa CDC also promoted the upcoming 5th Africa Public Health International Assembly (CPHIA 2026), to be held in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from November 23 to 27, 2026, through its public health conference platform, demonstrating the region's continued attention to public health issues and its intent for international cooperation.
  
  The WHO African Region's report further noted that although the number of health workers has increased, uneven distribution, skill structures that do not match needs, and the outflow of professionals to other countries continue to severely affect service delivery efficiency.
  
  The two reports echo each other in their core data, both confirming that Africa's health system faces a structural challenge of insufficient workforce. However, the WHO's announcement on the surveillance partnership did not explicitly name the seven participating countries, nor did it specify the funding scale or implementation timeline. In contrast, the health workforce report provided quantitative data, citing a 46% coverage rate and a total of 5.72 million workers, but did not detail differences between countries or priority intervention sequences. Additionally, the promotional information for CPHIA 2026 provided only the date and venue, without disclosing the conference agenda or expected outcomes, making it difficult for outsiders to assess its impact on actual policy or surveillance capabilities.
  
  These information gaps mean that although the cooperation framework has been established, execution details and effectiveness remain to be further observed and verified.
  
  The expanded implementation of this partnership will directly affect the epidemic surveillance capabilities and response efficiency of participating countries. For countries with high infectious disease incidence or weak medical infrastructure, the establishment of a collaborative surveillance system will help with early detection and control of outbreaks. The coexistence of shortages and unemployment revealed by the health workforce report reflects deeper issues in resource allocation and training systems. If these are not effectively addressed, they will limit the actual coverage of surveillance projects and medical services. For the African region, this move may promote the integration of resources between international and regional organizations, but it also highlights concerns about long-term dependence on external technical and financial support. If health worker training and retention mechanisms are not improved in tandem, the sustainability of surveillance systems will face challenges.
  
  The cooperation between the WHO and the Africa CDC is seen as a positive step forward in regional public health governance, but some observers point out that the effectiveness of the surveillance partnership depends on the political will and resource investment of individual governments. The selection criteria for the seven countries remain unclear, which could affect balanced regional development. The 46% coverage rate presented in the health workforce report, while reflecting a growth trend, may not solve grassroots healthcare accessibility issues if not accompanied by structural reforms; simply increasing the number of health workers may not be sufficient. Furthermore, although CPHIA 2026 provides an academic exchange platform, its specific outcomes and policy influence remain to be seen.
  
  The public should avoid overinterpreting any single data point or cooperation announcement and instead focus on subsequent implementation details and improvements in actual health indicators.
  
  Key areas for future observation will include the release of the list of seven participating countries, the specific indicators and funding sources for the surveillance projects, and long-term solutions to the health workforce shortage. Whether the WHO and Africa CDC will release more detailed implementation plans and progress reports will be crucial in assessing the effectiveness of this partnership. Meanwhile, the agenda and outcome documents of CPHIA 2026 may provide further context on regional public health priorities. The coexistence of unemployment and shortages revealed by the health workforce report is expected to become a core issue in future policy discussions. The formulation of related strategies on training, distribution, and retention will directly affect the resilience and sustainable development of Africa's public health system.

# 世衛與非洲疾控擴大監測夥伴關係，衛生人力短缺仍待解決

> 世衛組織、非洲疾控中心與德國RKI擴大「加強非洲疾病監測衛生安全夥伴關係」，於七國推動專案；同時報告顯示非洲衛生人力雖增長至572萬人，但仍僅達需求46%，短缺與失業並存。

世衛組織（WHO）近日宣佈與非洲疾病控制中心（Africa CDC）及德國羅伯特·科赫研究所（RKI）擴大既有夥伴關係，推動「加強非洲疾病監測衛生安全夥伴關係」（Partnership for Health Security Surveillance in Africa）於非洲大陸七個國家實施。此舉旨在強化區域內疫情監測與應變能力，回應非洲長期面臨的公共衛生挑戰。
與此同時，世衛組織非洲區域辦事處發布最新報告指出，非洲衛生人力規模已從2018年的430萬人增長至2024年的572萬人，但增長速度仍未能滿足實際需求，區域內僅有46%的衛生人力缺口獲補，且面臨嚴重的人力短缺與高失業率並存的矛盾現象。
根據世衛組織官方發布，此次擴大實施的夥伴關係聚焦於疾病監測系統的協作強化，透過整合世衛組織、非洲疾控中心與RKI的專業資源，在七個非洲國家推動具體專案。非洲疾控中心亦透過其公共衛生會議平臺宣傳即將於2026年11月23日至27日在衣索比亞亞迪斯阿貝巴舉辦的第五屆非洲公共衛生國際會議（CPHIA 2026），顯示區域內對公共衛生議題的持續關注與國際合作意圖。
世衛組織非洲區域辦事處的報告則進一步指出，儘管衛生人力數量有所增長，但分佈不均、技能結構不符需求，以及專業人才外流至其他國家等問題，仍嚴重影響服務供給效率。
兩項報導在核心資料上相互呼應，均確認非洲衛生體系正面臨人力不足的結構性挑戰。然而，世衛組織關於監測夥伴關係的發布未明確列出七個參與國家的名稱，亦未說明具體資金規模與執行時程；相對地，衛生人力報告提供了量化資料，指出46%的覆蓋率與572萬人的總數，但未詳細說明各國家間的差異與優先介入順序。此外，CPHIA 2026會議的宣傳資訊僅提供時間與地點，未披露會議議程或預期成果，使外界難以評估其對實際政策或監測能力的影響。
這些資訊缺口意味著，儘管合作框架已確立，但執行細節與成效仍待進一步觀察與驗證。
此項夥伴關係的擴大實施，將直接影響參與國的疫情監測能力與應變效率，尤其對於傳染病高發或醫療基礎設施較弱的國家而言，協作監測系統的建立有助於早期發現與控制疫情。衛生人力報告所揭示的短缺與失業並存現象，則反映出資源配置與培訓體系的深層問題，若未能有效解決，將限制監測專案與醫療服務的實際覆蓋範圍。對非洲區域而言，此舉可能促進國際組織與區域機構間的資源整合，但也凸顯出長期依賴外部技術與資金支援的隱憂。若人力培訓與留用機制未能同步完善，監測系統的永續性將面臨挑戰。
世衛組織與非洲疾控中心的合作被視為區域公共衛生治理的積極進展，但亦有觀點指出，監測夥伴關係的成效取決於各國政府的政治意願與資源投入，且七個國家的選擇標準未明，可能影響區域均衡發展。衛生人力報告所呈現的46%覆蓋率，雖反映增長趨勢，但若未伴隨結構性改革，單純增加人力數量未必能解決基層醫療可及性問題。此外，CPHIA 2026會議雖提供學術交流平臺，但其具體成果與政策影響力仍有待觀察。
外界應避免過度解讀單一資料或合作宣告，而需關注後續執行細節與實際健康指標的改善情況。
後續觀察重點將聚焦於七個參與國家的名單公佈、監測專案的具體指標與資金來源，以及衛生人力短缺問題的長期解決方案。世衛組織與非洲疾控中心是否會發布更詳細的執行計畫與進度報告，將是評估此夥伴關係成效的關鍵。同時，CPHIA 2026會議的議程與成果檔案，亦可能提供區域公共衛生優先事項的進一步脈絡。衛生人力報告所揭示的失業與短缺並存現象，預期將成為未來政策討論的核心議題，相關培訓、分佈與留用策略的制定，將直接影響非洲公共衛生體系的韌性與永續發展。
