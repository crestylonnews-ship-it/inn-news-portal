  # EU Promotes New Global Education Paradigm: Cross-Border Degree Recognition and Civic Literacy Emphasized Equally
  
  > The EU promotes global education through the European School Education Network, integrating the Bologna Process and credit transfer systems to strengthen European civic literacy and international degree recognition, with the Education University of Hong Kong also participating in related research collaboration.
  
  The European Union has long regarded education as a core policy area for deepening European integration and enhancing international competitiveness. Since the signing of the Maastricht Treaty in 1991, EU education policy has been clearly oriented toward establishing the "European Higher Education Area," advancing through phased initiatives such as the Socrates Programme to promote cross-border educational cooperation and student mobility.
  
  The cumulative effect of these treaties and programs laid the institutional foundation for the subsequent deepening of the Bologna Process, making the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS) a key mechanism for cross-border degree recognition.
  
  According to publicly available information on the EU School Education Network (school-education.ec.europa.eu), the EU positions "Global Education" as an emerging pedagogical paradigm, with its conceptual roots traceable to early initiatives by the Council of Europe. The webpage states that teachers today must prepare students for a global future; global education centers on European civic literacy while incorporating a worldwide perspective, aiming to address global challenges such as migration, racism, and discrimination by fostering values of inclusion, peace, and solidarity.
  
  This description aligns with the public introduction of the Global Education Research Institute (GRIFE) at the Education University of Hong Kong—which explicitly focuses on Finland, Europe, and countries of the Global South, emphasizes integrating global education concepts to cultivate future talent with adaptability and sustainable development awareness, and cites UNESCO's (2017) framework on critical thinking as its theoretical basis.
  
  However, different sources still present differences in the specific connotations and implementation pathways of global education that warrant further clarification. The EU School Education Network's introduction focuses on policy-level civic literacy development, emphasizing the combination of European citizenship and a global perspective; whereas the Education University of Hong Kong's GRIFE introduction leans more toward the practical dimensions of academic research and teacher education, centering on dialogue between the Finnish education model and countries of the Global South.
  
  Furthermore, materials from Taiwan's National Education Resources Center (teric.naer.edu.tw) note that one of the core objectives of the Bologna Process Declaration is precisely "to advance international reciprocity by recognizing and affirming qualifications obtained abroad," and to explicitly implement the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System, promoting the mutual recognition of qualifications earned by students across Europe, while certificates issued by teacher education institutions in each region will also be recognized. This passage complements the policy narrative of the EU School Education Network, yet also suggests that gaps in implementation and constraints from domestic laws across countries remain between degree recognition and educational paradigm transformation.
  
  In terms of concrete impact, the EU's promotion of global education and credit recognition mechanisms has already produced substantive effects on the operation of the European Higher Education Area. The convenience of cross-border student mobility has improved significantly, and course articulation and credit recognition among universities across countries have gradually become standardized. This has not only promoted educational integration within Europe but has also indirectly influenced the model of educational cooperation between the EU and countries of the Global South. The research direction of the Education University of Hong Kong's GRIFE precisely reflects the attention and response of Asian higher education institutions to changes in European education policy—through academic exchange with Nordic countries such as Finland, absorbing their educational innovation experiences and engaging in comparison and dialogue with the educational realities of countries in the Global South.
  
  From a different perspective, some scholars point out that although global education has received high priority at the policy level within the EU, its specific teaching practice remains constrained by each country's teacher training systems and curriculum design traditions. While the EU School Education Network's introduction emphasizes inclusion and peace, it does not elaborate in detail on how to specifically implement these principles in classrooms across different countries; meanwhile, GRIFE's introduction focuses on research and teacher education, with less attention to the execution details at the policy level.
  
  Additionally, the advancement of degree recognition mechanisms may also generate tensions between educational quality standardization and cultural diversity—while qualifications and certificates from various countries are being recognized, ensuring that educational content is not overly homogenized and respecting each country's educational traditions remain issues that have not yet been fully resolved.
  
  Key areas for future observation include: whether the EU will further revise global education teaching guidelines in the future to provide a more explicit framework for classroom practice; whether subsequent Bologna Process conferences will propose more binding regulations on the cross-border recognition of teacher education certificates; and whether the specific outcomes of the Education University of Hong Kong's GRIFE and its European cooperation networks will be translated into scalable teacher training models. These developments will determine the depth and breadth of global education's transition from policy advocacy to classroom practice.

# 歐盟推動全球教育新典範 跨國學歷互認與公民素養並重

> 歐盟透過歐洲學校教育網路推廣全球教育，結合波隆納程式與學分轉銜體系，強化歐洲公民素養與國際學歷互認，香港教育大學亦參與相關研究合作。

歐盟長期以來將教育視為深化歐洲一體化與提升國際競爭力的核心政策領域。自1991年《馬斯垂克條約》簽署後，歐盟教育政策的制訂明確朝向建立「歐洲高等教育區」（European Higher Education Area）的目標發展，並透過蘇格拉底計畫（Socrates Programme）等階段性行動，推動各國教育合作與學生流動。
此一系列條約與計畫的累積，為後續波隆納程式（Bologna Process）的深化奠定了制度基礎，也使歐洲學分轉銜體系（ECTS）成為跨國學歷承認的重要機制。
根據歐盟學校教育網路（school-education.ec.europa.eu）公開資料，歐盟將「全球教育」（Global Education）定位為一種新興的教學典範，其理念根源可追溯至歐洲理事會（Council of Europe）的早期倡議。該網頁指出，教師今日必須為學生的全球未來做好準備，全球教育以歐洲公民素養為核心，同時納入世界視野，旨在透過培養包容、和平與團結的價值觀，應對移民、種族主義與歧視等全球性挑戰。
此說法與香港教育大學全球教育研究所（GRIFE）的公開介紹相互呼應——該研究所明確以芬蘭、歐洲與全球南方國家為研究焦點，強調融合全球教育理念，培養具備適應力與永續發展意識的未來人才，並援引聯合國教科文組織（2017）關於批判性思維的框架作為理論依據。
然而，不同來源對全球教育的具體內涵與實施路徑，仍存在值得進一步釐清的差異。歐盟學校教育網路的介紹側重於政策層面的公民素養培育，強調歐洲公民身份與世界視野的結合；而香港教育大學 GRIFE 的介紹則更偏向學術研究與師資培育的實踐面向，將焦點放在芬蘭教育模式與全球南方國家的對話。
此外，臺灣國立教育資料館（teric.naer.edu.tw）所載的資料提及，波隆納程式宣言的核心目標之一，正是「邁向國際互惠，承認並肯定在國外所取得的學歷與資格」，並明確落實歐洲學分轉銜體系，促進全歐學生在各地所獲得的學歷得以獲得各國承認，同時各地師資培育機構所頒發的證書也將獲得承認。此一段落與歐盟學校教育網路的政策敘事形成互補，但也暗示了學歷互認與教育典範轉型之間，仍存在執行層面的落差與各國國內法的制約。
就具體影響而言，歐盟推動的全球教育與學分互認機制，已對歐洲高等教育區的運作產生實質作用。學生跨國流動的便利性大幅提升，各國大學之間的課程銜接與學分採認逐漸標準化，這不僅促進了歐洲內部的教育整合，也間接影響了歐盟與全球南方國家之間的教育合作模式。香港教育大學 GRIFE 的研究方向，正反映了亞洲高等教育機構對歐洲教育政策變遷的關注與回應——透過與芬蘭等北歐國家的學術交流，吸收其教育創新經驗，並將其與全球南方國家的教育現實進行比較與對話。
不同觀點方面，有學者指出，全球教育雖在政策層面獲得歐盟高度重視，但其具體教學實踐仍受限於各國教師培訓體系與課程設計傳統。歐盟學校教育網路的介紹雖強調包容與和平，但並未詳細說明如何在不同國家的課堂中具體落實；而 GRIFE 的介紹則側重於研究與師資培育，對政策層面的執行細節著墨較少。
此外，學歷互認機制的推進，亦可能引發關於教育品質標準化與文化多樣性之間的張力——當各國學歷與證書獲得承認的同時，如何確保教育內容不被過度統一、如何尊重各國教育傳統，仍是尚未完全解決的議題。
後續觀察重點包括：歐盟是否會在未來進一步修訂全球教育的教學指引，以提供更明確的課堂實踐框架；波隆納程式的後續會議是否會針對師資培育證書的跨國承認提出更具約束力的規範；以及香港教育大學 GRIFE 與歐洲合作網路的具體成果，是否會轉化為可推廣的師資培訓模式。這些進展將決定全球教育從政策倡議走向課堂實踐的深度與廣度。
